# E87 — 45 companies off the 1-of-3 frontier, and a source I refused for inverting its own meaning

2026-09-12, continuing E82–E86 in the same session. SHADOW throughout.

Three claims, 45 companies, every one from a regulator or a statistical agency. One of them also
closes `automotive_parts_equipment`'s rule-3 gap: until now its only two claims were its own
members' filings.

## The three

- **`automotive_parts_equipment` (16) — replication MODERATE.** 49 CFR 571.7: the Federal Motor
  Vehicle Safety Standards apply to *"items of motor vehicle equipment"*, not only whole
  vehicles, so an entrant into the regulated part of this unit must self-certify before it sells
  and can be recalled at its own cost if wrong. It binds most of these members — Autoliv's
  airbags and belts (FMVSS 208/209), Adient's and Lear's seats (207), Gentex's mirrors (111),
  Standard Motor Products and Dorman in the regulated aftermarket — and **not all** of them: Fox
  Factory's suspension, Patrick's RV components and XPEL's films are largely outside it, which
  the claim says. MODERATE, not HIGH: self-certification is a cost, not a licence — nobody
  approves the part in advance. And the barrier a Tier 1 would name first is the platform award
  and the tooling paid for it, which the claim records as **not yet evidenced by a non-issuer
  source**.
- **`health_care_services` (14) — replication MODERATE.** 42 CFR 424.505: *"a provider or
  supplier must be enrolled in the Medicare program."* Every provider type here reaches its
  customer through that gate, with a type-specific condition of participation on top (dialysis
  under 42 CFR 494, home health under 484, hospice under 418). Scope stated: Hims & Hers is
  cash-pay and Progyny employer-paid, so it binds roughly twelve of fourteen. What would make
  this HIGH is certificate-of-need law, which is a **state** matter with no single citable
  source — named as missing rather than assumed.
- **`civil_aerospace_platforms_components` (15) — growth MODERATE**, and this one carries a
  lesson for the whole corpus.

## Census M3 against the revenue route: the revenue route was two bands high

```text
Census M3, nondefense aircraft and parts shipments, year to date
  2026  $116,685m        2025  $108,152m        +7.9% nominal
  less CPI-U all items +3.4%                 = ~+4.5% real   -> MODERATE

the same unit via the members' own revenue (E83/E84 route)
  +18.5% nominal, +15.1% real                                -> EXCEPTIONAL
```

The agency's figure for what the industry actually shipped is **less than a third** of what the
members' own revenue implied, and the reason is visible once both are on the table: these fifteen
members include defence programmes, aftermarket services and engine maintenance that no
"nondefense aircraft and parts" line contains. **Their revenue and their industry's output are
different quantities.** The revenue route would have written EXCEPTIONAL where the published
quantity says MODERATE — a two-band error on the unit containing Boeing, RTX and GE Aerospace.

This is the strongest single piece of evidence this week for why rule 4 asks for a published
quantity, and it arrived by accident: the unit was refused by the 3-year concentration guard in
E84, so nothing was written, and the agency figure could be compared against what would have
been.

## The method note: attribution by arithmetic

M3's table is the same shape that made the Fed's G.17 unusable — a row of numbers whose header
did not survive PDF extraction. The difference is that **M3 prints the two levels next to their
percent change**, so `116,685 / 108,152 - 1 = +7.89%` reproduces the printed `7.9` and the column
attribution is established by arithmetic rather than by trusting a header through an extraction.
The G.17's industry rows are percentages only, with nothing to check them against.

**That is the actual dividing line between a citable table and an uncitable one**, and it is a
cheaper test than hunting for the header: if a row contains levels AND a change, the change
identifies the columns.

## The source I refused, and why it is the interesting refusal

Census's Monthly Construction Spending looked like it would answer `structural_growth` for five
or six REIT units at once. It publishes private construction put in place by property type, and
it self-verifies on the arithmetic test above:

```text
Office        140,064 vs 119,854   +16.9%   (private, +21.3% on the other series shown)
Commercial    122,510 vs 128,817    -4.9%
Lodging        24,668 vs  27,286    -9.6%
Health care    74,599 vs  76,420    -2.4%
```

It was not used, for two separate reasons.

**1. It measures supply additions, so for an asset owner it points the wrong way.** Construction
spending is how much new space is being built. For a REIT — which owns the existing stock —
*less* new construction means *less* competing supply and therefore firmer rents. Mapping
"lodging construction −9.6%" onto `structural_growth` DECLINING for hotel REITs would invert the
economics of the field. A supply measure is not a demand measure, and `structural_growth` is
asking about the industry's demand.

**2. The Office line contradicts everything else known about offices.** Private office
construction is up **21.3%** year over year in the same month that 35% of employed people work
from home (BLS, E86) and office REITs' own combined revenue reads negative in real terms. The
release breaks out no data-centre category and mentions neither "data center" nor "computer"
anywhere, so the most likely composition — data-centre construction sitting inside "Office" — is
something I **cannot confirm from the document**. An unattributable composition is the same
problem as an unattributable column.

So the REIT growth question stays open, and the five REIT units stay at 1 of 3. Recording the
refusal matters more than the gap: the next person to find this release will think it solves Real
Estate, and it does not.

**41 of the 49 remaining units hold five companies or fewer**, so the tail is long and thin:

blocked on the same thing — a published quantity for a property or travel sector that measures
**demand** rather than supply, and that covers the unit rather than one slice of it.
